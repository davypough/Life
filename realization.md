# Realization View

*A Functional Hierarchy of Life Processes*

This view presents one relation: between **mechanisms** and the **base capabilities and elaborations** they provide. It also introduces the two example systems used throughout the study. Base capabilities and elaborations are identified by what they achieve; this view records how they occur in particular systems. Mechanism and provides are defined in the Glossary.

## Diagram

```
Mechanism ──provides──▶ Base capability or elaboration
```

An arrow from M to C means **M provides C** in a particular system. The relation is many-to-many: one mechanism may provide several base capabilities or elaborations, and one may be provided by different mechanisms, or jointly by several.

## Example systems

Two systems illustrate the base capabilities and elaborations throughout the study: a hypothetical A/B network for primitive examples, and a living cell for developed ones. Both are realizations; the accounts in Base Capabilities and Elaborations describe how each provides a given base capability or elaboration.

### A primitive A/B network example

- A hypothetical network in which A and B help produce one another illustrates organizational regeneration.
- Variants of the network illustrate elaborations and combinations. A variant that adds only what one base capability or elaboration needs is a minimal realization of it; a variant that combines several is not minimal. Each added part is introduced in the account it illustrates.
- Each variant states which conditions are met by the network and which by its surroundings.
- The examples illustrate proposed possibilities; their order of presentation does not establish a dependency chain or an evolutionary history.
- A part used in one variant need not be present in another; combined examples do not establish that every included base capability or elaboration is a prerequisite.

```
┌─ Environment (e.g., a mineral pore) ────────────────────┐
│                                                         │
│  supplies energy-rich resources (R);                    │
│  confines the network's reactive components (R,A,B)     │
│                                                         │
│     ┌─ A/B network ───────────────────────────────┐     │
│     │                                             │     │
│     │                    A                        │     │
│     │          R ─────────────────▶ B             │     │
│     │                                             │     │
│     │                    B                        │     │
│     │          R ─────────────────▶ A             │     │
│     │                                             │     │
│     └─────────────────────────────────────────────┘     │
│                                                         │
│  receives losses of A and B (decay, dispersal);         │
│  carries away residue (by-product)                      │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

A catalyst written over an arrow helps the reaction without being consumed. Each reaction's product is the other's catalyst, so the network renews both, while the environment meets every other support condition. R also carries the usable energy: its conversion to A and B is energetically favorable, so no separate energy source is needed. Each conversion also releases a small by-product, residue, which leaves the pore as R arrives (see Elaborations, *Waste management*).

The network is a two-member autocatalytic set in the sense of Kauffman,[^kauffman] and a RAF set in the sense of Hordijk and Steel,[^hordijk] with R as the supplied "food set". Lincoln and Joyce[^lincoln2009] built a laboratory analogue: two RNA enzymes, each assembling the other from supplied building blocks. Their system also copies by template, which the A/B network does not. It is an actual realization of regeneration with template heredity in which the environment meets every other support condition, with no individuation: serial transfer to fresh supplies, done by the experimenters, meets separation and dispersal. It is therefore a living system in the study's sense, though not in ordinary usage, an accepted consequence of the criterion (Basis, *Sufficiency of the criterion*).

### A complex living cell example

A present-day cell, such as a bacterium, provides the developed examples. Its base capabilities and elaborations are tightly integrated, and many are provided by shared mechanisms such as the membrane (see *Observations*). It shows how base capabilities and elaborations occur in a developed system, not what each requires; minimal realizations serve that purpose (Method, *Minimal realizations*).

## Scope

- Entries are limited to the two example systems. They illustrate the relation; they are not a catalogue of biological mechanisms.
- Mechanisms are named at the grain at which they provide something, such as "membrane" or "transport proteins", not at the level of their molecular detail.
- The accounts of how each mechanism works remain in Base Capabilities and Elaborations; this view holds only the mapping and what follows from it.

## Potential mechanisms in the A/B network

| Mechanism | Provides |
|---|---|
| A and B making each other from R | Organizational regeneration |
| A jelly-like material that holds A and B together | Individuation |
| The jelly capturing R where A and B can use it | Resource provisioning |
| A coupling component using light to turn less reactive R into activated R, which A and B convert | Energy transduction (the pore holds the activated R near A and B) |
| Excess A inhibiting B | Self-regulation |
| A pigment, made from R, absorbing UV in the pore before it reaches A and B | Environmental protection (the pore holds the pigment) |
| A converting component, made from R, turning residue into an inert form | Waste management |
| A heating component, made from R, breaking R down to residue and releasing its energy as heat | Heat gain (a large, poorly conducting pore retains the heat) |
| A glassy component, made from R, setting around A and B as the pore dries and dissolving when water returns | Dormancy (the pore holds A and B together through the dry interval) |
| A sensing component binding incoming R and speeding up production of A (free in the pore, or at the surface of the jelly in a combined variant) | Environmental responsiveness |
| A and B carried by slow diffusion into a vacant neighboring pore, founding a network that grows from them | Reproduction (the separation itself is met by the pore geometry) |
| A and B in still water forming a spot that grows and splits as its center is depleted of R | Reproduction (separation met by production and diffusion rates in the still medium) |
| Production of jelly, A, and B in excess of losses, so that the blob of jelly grows and breaks into fragments that each continue | Reproduction, in a variant combined with individuation (the breaking itself is done by disturbance in the surroundings) |
| A linking component, made from R, binding A to B in pairs, so that any founding set containing a pair is complete | Partitioning |
| A releasing component that loosens mineral grains carrying A and B from the pore wall, so that the flow carries them to vacant pores | Dispersal (the grains keep the founding set together) |
| A variant of A, regenerated by the network and carried into new networks | Heredity (compositional inheritance) |
| In a template variant, A and B as complementary strands, each joining pieces made from R into the other | Heredity (template inheritance) |
| An editing component that pulls pieces held mismatched on a template apart before they are joined | Error correction (in the template variant) |
| The releasing component of dispersal, when grains carrying a variant of A reach an occupied pore, whose network regenerates the variant alongside its own components | Recombination (exchange is occasional, so organizational separation holds) |

| Mechanism | Provides |
|---|---|
| Metabolic network producing catalysts, membrane, and genome | Organizational regeneration |
| Membrane | Individuation |
| Transport proteins in the membrane | Resource provisioning (jointly with the membrane) |
| ATP synthase driven by an ion gradient across the membrane | Energy transduction (jointly with the membrane) |
| Glycolysis making ATP from glucose (substrate-level phosphorylation) | Energy transduction |
| Photosynthetic reaction center converting the excitation of its pigment into separated charges | Energy transduction (jointly with the membrane) |
| ATP, charged by transducing mechanisms and consumed by many production steps | Energy transduction (shared carrier; see *Observations*) |
| Light-harvesting antenna (chlorophylls and carotenoids) | Resource provisioning, gathering light to the reaction center |
| Carotenoids | Resource provisioning (harvesting light); environmental protection (dissipating excess light); waste management (quenching singlet oxygen from the cell's own photosynthesis) |
| Feedback inhibition of enzymes; regulation of gene expression by metabolite levels | Self-regulation |
| Membrane receptors | Environmental responsiveness |
| UV-absorbing pigments (scytonemin, mycosporine-like amino acids); S-layer; outer membrane | Environmental protection (the outer membrane jointly with individuation) |
| Catalase and superoxide dismutase | Environmental protection against outside oxidants; waste management of reactive oxygen from the cell's own metabolism |
| Efflux pumps | Environmental protection when exporting outside toxins; waste management when exporting the cell's own by-products |

| Membrane pyrophosphatase pumping protons as it splits pyrophosphate | Waste management; energy transduction, capturing the energy in a gradient |

| Flagellar motility | Dispersal; also resource provisioning when it moves the cell toward resources |

| Meiosis and fertilization | Recombination combined with reproduction (sexual reproduction) |

## Observations

- **One mechanism can provide several elaborations.** The jelly provides both individuation and provisioning in the A/B network. The releasing component provides dispersal when grains reach a vacant pore and recombination when they reach an occupied one; in cells, homologous recombination provides both recombination and error correction. Catalase, efflux pumps, and carotenoids provide environmental protection or waste management depending on the source of what they act on: an outside influence or the cell's own by-product (Glossary, *By-product*). Like evaporative cooling acting on heat (Elaborations, *Heat gain*), they act on a chemical species whatever its source, so where both sources are present they provide both in proportion. In a collective, a member's catalase acting on a neighbor's peroxide provides protection for the member and waste management for the collective. This differs from the environment view's observation that one elaboration can meet several support conditions: here the sharing is between elaborations, at the level of means.
- **One base capability or elaboration can be provided by different mechanisms.** Individuation is provided by the jelly or by a membrane; reproduction by spreading into a neighboring pore, by spot splitting, by fragmentation, by an overgrown membrane, or by division machinery; heredity by composition or by template copying. This is what Method means by distinguishing a base capability or elaboration from the physical means that provide it.
- **Several mechanisms can jointly provide one elaboration.** In the cell, provisioning needs both transport proteins and the membrane that holds them. Joint provision differs from a combination (Glossary): a combination is several elaborations operating together; joint provision is several mechanisms providing one.
- **Some relations hold only for particular realizations.** In chemiosmotic cells, energy transduction relies on the membrane, because ATP synthase is driven by a gradient the membrane maintains. That is a relation between mechanisms in particular realizations, not between elaborations: fermentation makes ATP with no gradient, and a cell-free yeast extract ferments sugar with no boundary at all;[^buchner1897] the coupling component in the A/B network needs no boundary; and natural gradients across mineral walls, as proposed for alkaline hydrothermal vents, would let a system transduce across a boundary it does not produce.[^lanemartin2012] Fermenting cells still have membranes, so fermentation in cells separates transduction from the gradient, not from individuation; the extract and the A/B network separate it from individuation. Method requires such relations to be recorded with the realization for which they hold; this view is where they are recorded. They enter the dependency view only if shown to hold across the stated range of realizations.
- **Integration in developed systems comes from shared mechanisms.** The cell's membrane takes part in individuation, provisioning, transduction, and reproduction. A shared carrier such as ATP integrates in the same way, linking many transducing mechanisms to many consumers, including the means of other elaborations. Developed systems therefore look as if their elaborations depend on one another, even where minimal realizations show they need not.
- **Some couplings recur in every known living system.** Dormancy, heat gain, and dispersal each occur with individuation in every known case: spores, tuns, and seeds are individuated; heat gain occurs only in large individuated bodies, since a single cell cannot warm itself (Elaborations, *Heat gain*); and dispersing units are cells, seeds, or fragments held by a matrix. Their dissociations are hypothetical only: a drying pore holding A and B, a large pore in poorly conducting rock, mineral grains carrying a founding set. No physical argument makes any of them depend on individuation, so each pair stays unordered and is recorded here as a coupling (Method, *Checks against biology*). By contrast, transduction, self-regulation, and environmental responsiveness are shown working without a boundary in vitro: in cell-free fermentation, in purified enzymes inhibited by their pathway's end product,[^gerhart1962] and in RNA enzymes switched by a bound ligand in free solution.[^tang1997]
- **Means made jointly by several systems belong to the collective.** Organizational separation is assessed for a stated organization (Glossary, *Organizational separation*). A biofilm matrix is renewed mainly by cells other than any given one, so for each cell's own organization it is mainly environment, with that cell's contribution in part. For the organization of cells and matrix together, the collective renews the matrix from its own production, so it is the collective's means, providing individuation, provisioning, and protection of the collective. In the A/B network, networks in linked pores whose jelly merges are several systems for A and B but one for the jelly. The two descriptions concern different organizations and do not conflict; what follows for nesting is in Basis, *Graded and nested living systems*.
- **Members can be mechanisms of a collective.** Member types can jointly provide a collective's elaborations, as in division of labor, and a member's death or reproduction can itself be the means, as stalk cells die lifting a Dictyostelium slug's spores and lymphocytes multiply in clonal selection. Each member regenerates its own organization while it lives; for the collective's organization, it is a component the collective renews (Elaborations, *Candidates assessed*).
- **Every mechanism is itself produced and renewed by the network.** This is the mechanism-level counterpart of each elaboration's prerequisite that it elaborates organizational regeneration.

## What the view claims and does not claim

- It shows how base capabilities and elaborations can occur, not that any mechanism is necessary for them.
- It says nothing about the order in which mechanisms arose. Sharing a mechanism in a developed system is not evidence of historical sequence.
- The absence of a mechanism for an elaboration in either system means only that none has been specified, not that none is possible.

## Open questions

1. Grain: if two candidate elaborations cannot be provided by separate mechanisms in any realization, should they be combined? If one is provided by unrelated mechanisms across many realizations, does that confirm it as a distinct contribution? Closed: both are evidence for the grain test, not tests. Provision never by separate mechanisms can arise only where the takeover test already joins two contributions, and provision by unrelated mechanisms shows that an entry is identified by its contribution, not that it is distinct from others (Method, *Identifying and naming base capabilities and elaborations*).
2. When a coupling between mechanisms recurs in every known realization, what would turn it into a physical dependency between elaborations? Closed: a physical argument that one contribution cannot be made without the other, with every proposed dissociation failing on physical grounds; co-occurrence alone never suffices (Method, *Checks against biology*). Current couplings are recorded in *Observations*.
